Thinking about thought

Nobody was first. Several literate civilisations turned toward the mind within a few centuries of each other, took different roads, and largely did not compare notes until the nineteenth century. Hover any point for what happened and where to read it.

The entries

What the picture flattens

The dates are not equally solid. The Indian column is the shakiest: almost nothing there is firmly datable, most texts have no single author, and scholarly ranges run to centuries. The Greek and Chinese entries are anchored to individuals and fare somewhat better. Treat every date here as the midpoint of an argument, not a fact.

The scale is compressed. The horizontal axis is deliberately non-linear — the centuries between 600 and 200 BCE are stretched, because that is where nearly everything happens. The shaded band marks that stretch, the span Karl Jaspers labelled the axial age. The label is worth using loosely: the clustering is real, the explanation for it is not settled.

The lanes suggest more separateness than there was. After Alexander there was genuine Greco-Indian contact, though how much philosophical traffic crossed it remains argued. And within India the standard story — Veda, then Upanishad, then Buddhism, one tradition deepening — has been challenged by Johannes Bronkhorst, who argues that asceticism, karma and rebirth were native to a cultural region east of the Vedic heartland and were absorbed into Brahmanical thought rather than generated by it.

Two very different methods sit side by side here. Greece approached the mind through language and public argument: thinking is inner speech, so you analyse it as you would analyse speech, and you test claims by debate. India approached it through trained attention: you stabilise the mind first, then look. Both are sophisticated, and they see different things — which is why Greek philosophy is superb on inference and judgment and comparatively thin on moment-to-moment phenomenology, and the Abhidhamma is the reverse.
